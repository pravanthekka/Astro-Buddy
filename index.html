    Project story

    Team The Sibling Circuit · NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 · Challenge: create health-monitoring software for astronauts on space missions.
    Team members: Pravanthekka GN and Pravarthanan GN.

    What we built

    AstroBuddy is a calm health companion concept for astronauts on long-duration missions. A wearable band and radiation badge are envisioned to provide health and exposure readings automatically, reducing the need for manual data entry.

    The software prototype displays changing readings, identifies values that cross illustrative thresholds, explains the situation in plain language and suggests possible next steps.

    This page demonstrates the concept using a simulated stream from fictional crew. It is not connected to a physical wearable device.

    The problem

    On long missions, astronauts work far from Earth in an environment involving radiation, isolation, altered gravity and limited access to medical support. They must manage demanding mission tasks while also paying attention to their health.

    A monitoring tool should make health information easier to understand without creating unnecessary workload or panic. It should help crew members recognise changes and know when to seek medical support.

    How it meets the challenge

    AstroBuddy explores how a single onboard interface could bring several health indicators together and present understandable guidance.

    Impact

    • Brings several health indicators together in one interface.
    • Turns example readings into clear status levels and suggested actions.
    • Uses calm language instead of presenting numbers without context.
    • Explores how onboard decision-support software could help crews working far from Earth.
    • The underlying idea may also inspire monitoring tools for people in remote environments, although this would require separate research and testing.

    What makes AstroBuddy different

    • Calm by design. The interface pairs a status with an explanation and suggested next steps.
    • Quiet when steady. When readings are within the prototype's illustrative ranges, the dashboard stays reassuring and unobtrusive.
    • Multiple indicators. The concept brings cardiovascular, sleep, activity, oxygen, glucose and radiation information together.
    • Built-in breathing guide. A simple breathing animation demonstrates a possible well-being support feature.

    Prototype testing

    We explored the prototype using five simulated health scenarios: a normal day, low blood pressure, breathing difficulty, low glucose and elevated radiation exposure.

    For each scenario, we checked how the dashboard changes its status, communicates the concern and presents suggested next steps.

    This is an initial functional demonstration, not a clinical trial or astronaut user study. The next stage would be usability testing with users and feedback from medical and space-health experts.

    Current capabilities and limitations

    What this prototype demonstrates:

    • A simulated stream of health indicators for fictional crew members.
    • A dashboard showing heart rate, blood oxygen, blood pressure, sleep, glucose, stress estimates, exercise and radiation exposure.
    • Illustrative status levels: Steady, Watch and Act Now.
    • Example guidance for different simulated scenarios.
    • A breathing guide and demonstration alert controls.

    What is not yet implemented:

    • No physical wearable device is connected.
    • No real astronaut or patient data is used.
    • The alert thresholds are illustrative, not NASA-approved medical thresholds.
    • The medical alert and message buttons are demonstrations. They do not send messages to an actual medical officer or Earth.
    • The prototype has not undergone clinical validation or astronaut testing.

    AstroBuddy is an early decision-support prototype, not a medical device or a replacement for professional medical judgement. A real mission system would require validated sensors, medically reviewed algorithms, privacy safeguards and extensive testing.

    Next steps

    • Explore how personal baselines could make monitoring more relevant to each crew member.
    • Develop longer-term trend analysis for sleep, activity and other indicators.
    • Investigate additional health indicators relevant to long-duration spaceflight.
    • Explore integration with suitable wearable sensors and radiation dosimeters.
    • Seek feedback from medical professionals and space-health researchers before any real-world use.
