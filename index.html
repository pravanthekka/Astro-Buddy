    About AstroBuddy

    Team The Sibling Circuit · NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 · Challenge: build health monitoring software for astronauts on long missions.
    Team members: Pravanthekka GN and Pravarthanan GN.

    What we built

    AstroBuddy is a calm health-monitoring software concept for astronauts on long-duration missions. It explores how wearable health indicators and radiation readings could be presented in one easy-to-understand dashboard.

    When a reading moves outside an illustrative range, the prototype explains the concern and presents possible next steps in plain language. Its goal is to make health information easier to understand without adding unnecessary stress.

    It is designed to sit on top of wearable sensing that already exists, such as the Canadian Space Agency’s Bio-Monitor smart shirt, and adds a calm guidance layer for the crew member. It also includes an optional, private cycle-tracker demo for crew members who menstruate.

    The current website is a functional demonstration using simulated readings and fictional crew members.

    The problem

    During long-duration missions, astronauts operate far from Earth and have limited access to immediate medical support. Spaceflight can expose crew members to challenges such as radiation, isolation, disrupted sleep and changes associated with living in microgravity.

    Astronauts also have demanding responsibilities. A health-support system should therefore make important information easy to understand and help identify situations that may need medical review.

    Women’s health deserves attention too. Most female astronauts currently suppress their periods on missions, but research on how long missions affect the menstrual cycle is limited, and some astronauts may prefer to keep menstruating on missions to the Moon and Mars.

    How it addresses the challenge

    The concept brings several health indicators together in one dashboard. The current prototype demonstrates the following areas:

    Health areaPrototype feature
    Cardiovascular healthIllustrative blood pressure and heart-rate readings
    Oxygen levelsSimulated blood-oxygen readings and guidance
    Blood glucoseSimulated glucose readings and suggested next steps
    Radiation exposureSample daily dose readings and shelter guidance
    Behavioural healthStress estimate and a calm-breathing guide
    Sleep and exerciseSleep duration and strength-exercise tracking
    Women’s healthOptional, private cycle-tracker demo using fictional data
    Immune changesNot yet included; identified as a future development area

    Expected impact

    • Presents multiple health indicators in one place, making them easier to review.
    • Uses calm, plain-language messages instead of showing numbers alone.
    • Demonstrates how unusual readings could be flagged for medical review.
    • Supports an inclusive approach to astronaut health, including optional menstrual-cycle tracking.
    • Explores a possible approach that could also be useful for people working in remote environments on Earth.

    These are intended benefits of the concept, not outcomes established through clinical testing.

    What makes AstroBuddy different?

    • Calm by design. It pairs a status message with suggested next steps.
    • One dashboard. It brings several simulated health indicators together for easier review.
    • Quiet when steady. It highlights readings that may need attention rather than overwhelming the user with constant messages.
    • Breathing support. A simple breathing guide is included as a demonstration feature.

    Prototype testing

    We explored the prototype using five simulated health scenarios: a normal day, low blood pressure, breathing difficulty, low glucose and elevated radiation exposure.

    For each scenario, we checked how the dashboard changes its status, communicates the concern and presents suggested next steps.

    This is an initial functional demonstration, not a clinical trial or astronaut user study. Further usability testing and expert review would be needed.

    Honest limits

    • All readings are simulated, and the crew are fictional.
    • Ranges are illustrative. A real system would require medically validated thresholds, appropriate sensor selection and clinical review.
    • Some signals, such as cuffless blood pressure, are difficult to measure reliably on the wrist today. A real system would need suitable, validated sensors for each measurement.
    • The cycle tracker uses fictional sample data and gives no medical advice.
    • This is decision support, not a doctor. Serious or unclear signs would need assessment by qualified medical personnel.
    • The alert and message buttons are demonstrations only. They do not send real notifications to a medical officer, flight surgeon or Earth.

    Future possibilities

    • Explore integration with suitable wearable sensors and radiation monitoring devices.
    • Study individual health baselines and long-term trends.
    • Investigate additional indicators, including immune-related changes.
    • Support astronauts’ choices on menstrual health, whether they track their cycle or suppress it.
    • Develop a secure communication pathway for medical review.
    • Seek feedback from medical professionals and conduct usability testing before considering real-world use.
